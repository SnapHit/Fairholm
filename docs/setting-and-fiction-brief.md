## 1. The pitch in one paragraph

You have taken passage to a new world under a company charter. The passage was financed, and that debt
is the first thing you owe. The world is generous, beautiful and better than the one you left.
Everything hard about your life comes from the terms: the Company owns the return freight and prices
what you sell, and sells you the things you cannot yet make at a price that climbs every year. It will
gladly sell you machines too, along with the spares that keep them alive, and every one of those is a
rope. You build, you refine, you learn to make what you were buying, and eventually you can supply
yourself faster than the ships can. Then you say so, and the Company sends a fleet, and it takes them a
year to arrive.

---

## 2. The historical analogue is Australia, not America

A deliberate correction to the obvious frame.

The American colonial story has independence built into its first scene, so the arc is predetermined
and the player is only executing it. The Australian one has the dependence problem in a far more
useful form: the first years were genuinely fed from home, the distance was absurd, and what broke the
dependence was not ideology but competence. The colonies became better at feeding and supplying
themselves than the ships were, and independence followed the economy rather than leading it.

**That is the fourth ratchet, earned rather than announced.**

Two other properties transfer directly:

- A new world of plenty and vast space, where the early stages are brutal and the established colonies
  do very well
- A gold rush that transforms a region, then depletes, while the lasting wealth turns out to be in
  wool, wheat and manufacturing

Note for honesty: the real continent was already inhabited, and that history is contested, ongoing and
local to the author. The decision in section 9 exists precisely so that no trade table in this game is
a portrait of Aboriginal people.

---

## 3. The world: generous, beautiful, and earthlike

**A hard art and design constraint, not a mood note.**

- The planet is survivable without technology. Breathable, temperate enough, no suits, no airlocks, no
  interiors-only gameplay
- It is more beautiful than the world the player left, and the migration should feel worth it even
  while the ledger is grim
- **All hardship comes from the terms, never from the planet.** A world trying to kill you cannot
  sustain wonder, and the game becomes a survival simulator, which is a different game for a different
  audience
- **Earthlike, not alien.** Settled in version 2. "Alien" in practice means arbitrary, and arbitrary is
  both unpaintable and unlearnable. The land should read as unfamiliar country rather than another
  planet: a coherent, slightly displaced palette, plausible geology, growing things that are variations
  on shapes the eye already parses. This is also far cheaper, because every form can be a variant of
  something known
- **Not dated.** Never name a century or a year. Naming one invites the player to audit the technology
  against it. Long enough from now that the crossing is possible, close enough that nothing else has
  changed

This is why Mars was rejected. Mars supplies airlocks, rationing and triage, and nothing worth shipping
home. It is also crowded, including by an economic game with a crashable commodity market designed by
a Civilization lead.

Any wonder the world contains must be load-bearing, meaning it does a job in the mechanics, or it will
be cut the first time the economy needs tuning.

---

## 4. The player's position: shareholder-settler, not the Company

**The player is not the corporation and never plays as the corporation.** This is the difference
between this game and the obvious science fiction reference points, which are stories about employees.

- The Company financed the passage. That debt is the tax
- The Company holds the monopoly on return freight, which is why the export price is theirs to set
- The Company sells what cannot yet be made locally, which is why the import price climbs
- Everything the player builds, the player owns
- The revolt is the moment the player no longer needs the freight or the supply

The emigration fantasy stays with the player: arriving with nothing, building something, becoming
untouchable.

---

## 5. The four ratchets, mapped onto this fiction

| Ratchet | Fiction |
|---|---|
| Export prices fall with cumulative volume | The Company's freight monopoly, and a finite Earth appetite for raw cargo |
| Import prices drift up | Precision goods, spares and consumables that cannot be made locally at first |
| Extraction rises | The passage debt and its charges, which only ever increase |
| The rebellion metric arms the opponent | Recorded grievance travels home with the freight |

**The chokepoint import is capability, not survival.** The player would live perfectly well without it.
They would simply stay small forever. That is a far better argument for independence than suffocation,
and it lets the world stay beautiful for the whole game.

## 6. Technology, and why the colony looks the way it does

**This section resolves the central tension in the setting and everything visual follows from it.**

### Knowledge is weightless. Capability is not.

Every colonist knows what a bearing is, what a transistor is, how a generator works. None of it helps.
A bearing needs precision machining, which needs machine tools, which need precision measurement, which
needs a metrology base, which needs a chemical industry. The pyramid is thousands of layers deep and
every layer physically requires the ones beneath it.

**An industrial base is an object, not a piece of information, and it cannot be shipped.**

So what crosses is what is light, durable, and either self-replicating or irreplaceable:

- people, and the knowledge in their heads
- seeds and livestock, which reproduce
- hand tools, which last
- a small number of precision items that cannot be made locally at any price

What does not cross is anything heavy relative to its value, and anything that needs a live supply
chain to keep working.

### A machine you cannot mend is a subscription

**Advanced technology is absolutely available.** The Company will happily sell you a machine. It will
sell you the spares and the consumables too, at a price that climbs every year, and you will need them,
because a machine that arrives is a machine that will eventually fail with nothing local able to repair
it.

**Every imported machine is therefore a rope, not a gift.** The settlement that takes them goes faster
and owes more. The settlement that refuses them goes slower and owes nothing.

**So the pre-industrial look is a choice, not a limitation.** The settlements built in timber, stone
and animal power are not backward. They are the ones that can maintain everything they own. That is a
far more interesting thing to look at than a colony that simply cannot afford better, and it is the
argument of the game rendered in silhouettes.

### The visible arc

The mechanical progression and the visual progression are the same thing, which costs nothing extra
because the building tiers exist anyway.

- **Early.** Timber, thatch, hand tools, sail, animal haulage. Rough and small
- **Middle.** Worked stone, wheels, water and wind power, larger boats, the first bought machines and
  the dependency that comes with them
- **Late.** Your own tooling industry, so chimneys, metal, and the first machines you can actually
  mend. What you build now, you keep

### The historical precedent, which is exact

This is not an invented arrangement. Britain's Iron Act of 1750 removed duties on colonial pig and bar
iron entering England while prohibiting the erection of new colonial mills for slitting or rolling
iron, plating forges and steel furnaces. Raw export encouraged, local finishing forbidden. That is the
Company's freight monopoly and its tooling trade, already documented.

### Two assumptions this rests on, which must never be contradicted

1. **The crossing is genuinely marginal.** Barely worth doing, justified only by the headline resource.
   If ships were cheap the whole structure collapses, because then you simply ship an industrial base
2. **General-purpose self-replicating manufacturing does not work.** If it did, the Company would send
   machines instead of people, and there would be no colony and no game

## 7. How anything gets here: the lander

**Settled 22 August 2026.** This was previously left vague and the vagueness was causing problems, most
visibly the question of why ships approach the coast at all on a world reached by spacecraft.

### The arrangement

**The crossing vessel never lands.** It arrives, holds off the planet, and leaves. Nobody in Fairholm
ever sees it.

**What comes down is a lander**, a heavy capsule that **splashes down at sea**. Water landing is not a
flourish; it is the cheapest way to put mass on a planet that has an ocean. No landing field, no
infrastructure, no prepared ground, and the water absorbs the energy. On a crossing this marginal,
cheapness decides everything.

**The lander is the first ship.** It comes down in open sea, in fog, out of sight of land but near
enough to a coast with food, timber and fresh water that sailing in any sensible direction sights land
within the first move or two, carrying the starting colonists and knowing nothing of the world. It
moves wherever boats can go, open water and the major rivers, six tiles a turn, and sees three tiles
around it; a hold plots a course through the fog and a tap on the course's end, or Go, sails it, and a
course that meets land stops at the coast. The opening turns are for choosing ground, not for reaching it: you sight land quickly, run
along the coast comparing sites, and choose your own ground. Rival charters' landers sail at the same
speed. There is no landing-site chooser: nobody tells you where to land.

**The lander founds the first settlement and is consumed doing it.** It beaches on any legal land tile
beside it and everyone aboard becomes the settlement's people; it must have at least one colonist
aboard to do so. Colonists may go ashore to scout on foot, but nobody founds anything until the lander
has, so the first settlement is always on the water and trade works from day one. Legal ground is
nearly everywhere: any tile that is not mountain or water, with at least two clear tiles between it and
the centre of any existing settlement, anyone's. The lander also carries a boat, which survives once
the lander is gone as your first working ship, a lighter, on the water alongside. The lighter sails at
a lighter's pace, and the drop from a fast machine to a slow sail is intended.

**The first lander is never recovered.** Beached, it is the settlement. The Company writes it off
against the passage debt, which is part of why that debt is so large. **So the first metal in the
colony is the thing that brought you, and you are still paying for it.**

**Freight landers** come down offshore of any coastal settlement you own, are emptied, and are broken
up. An inland settlement hauls to a coastal one.

### What this settles

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Why boats matter from turn one | Because you begin at sea, and the lander that brings you is your first ship |
| Why the first settlement is always on the water | Only the lander can found it, and the lander beaches from the sea or a major river |
| Why the recall fleet approaches by sea | Its landers splash down offshore and motor in to any coast within two tiles of one of your settlements |
| Why the approach narrows over three turns | Splashdown is visible whatever the fog hides, and the landers take three turns to motor in, so the stretch of coast they could reach narrows each turn. See section 10 of the military brief |
| Why a blockade strangles | Cargo has to reach the water to get off-world at all, and a hostile armed ship on the water beside a settlement closes it |
| Why no spacecraft is ever rendered closely | A splashdown is a mark on the water and a plume of steam |

**The Landing** is the default name of the first settlement, and nothing more: the place where the
lander beached. It is not a fixed splashdown zone and not a shore facility, and there are no anchorages.
Cargo reaches the water at any coastal settlement you own, ships unload onto any coastal land tile, and
a coastal settlement works its water tiles without a wharf; the wharf, drydock and shipyard remain for
building and repairing ships. Every coastal settlement is a door, which is exactly what makes shutting
one serious.

### The two transport layers

They remain completely different systems and should never be confused.

| | The crossing | On-planet haulage |
|---|---|---|
| Who operates it | The Company. The player only consigns to it | The player |
| Duration | Months each way | Turns |
| What the player sees | A splashdown in fog at the start, and later freight landers splashing down off a coastal settlement, unloading, and being broken up | Boats, haulers, roads, wharves |
| Mechanical role | Price lag, warehouse pressure, and why the recall fleet is a gamble | The logistics game |

**The science fiction lives in the arrangement, never in the texture.** Keeping the vessel unseen and
the lander simple means never rendering the one reference silhouette every player would judge, which is
the asset argument in section 12 of the architecture brief doing useful work.

### One consequence for the consignment office

If cargo must physically reach the water at a coastal settlement, an inland consignment office would
seem useless under blockade. It is not, because **it is a contractual device rather than a warehouse**.
Goods are consigned to the Company's account and their agents arrange carriage at their own risk.
Inland it cannot be seized or blockaded directly, and during a blockade it works at a smuggler's rate
because that carriage becomes dangerous.

## 7a. The first ship, and why you were sent

**Settled 1 October 2026.** The backstory is five sentences long, and that is deliberate.

The first ship took a hundred years to reach this world. Its people found Bloom, a flower that grows
nowhere else, and from it they made cores, and cores cut the crossing to eight months. They shipped
cores home. Then they went silent, and the cores stopped coming. The Company has sent you on what cores
it had left, to start again.

This is the root of three things already in this document: why the crossing is marginal (section 6),
why cores are what the Company cannot do without (section 8), and why the predecessors know the ground
and you do not (section 9).

The five lines below are canonical and are what the player reads at the start, fading in one at a time
over the splashdown until the first tap dismisses them.

> "The first ship took a hundred years to reach this world."
>
> "Its people found Bloom, a flower that grows nowhere else."
>
> "From it they made cores, and cores cut the crossing to eight months."
>
> "Then they went silent, and the cores stopped coming."
>
> "The Company has sent you to start again. No one is waiting."

## 8. Resources: gold is the trap, refining is the engine

**Gold stays gold and remains the trap, deliberately.** Do not fix it. The Victorian goldfields are the
same shape in real history: a spectacular surface phase, depletion, and the lasting money made by the
people who stayed and manufactured.

1. **Gold buys tempo, not compounding.** Passage for people, tooling, rushed construction. All
   one-offs. The refining chains compound and gold does not, and the player learns this by feel
2. **The crash is about demand as well as depletion.** Earth's appetite for the metal is finite while
   its appetite for refined goods grows

### What the crossing runs on

**Settled 21 August 2026.** Bloom is distilled into attar; attar and metal together make **cores**, and
**cores are what makes the crossing possible. They are consumed by it.**

- It gives the Company a reason to tolerate a colony it cannot control
- Earth's demand is structural rather than commercial, so unlike gold it never saturates, which is the
  finite-versus-growing distinction below made concrete
- The colonial loop becomes exact: Fairholm manufactures the means of its own extraction
- And it gives the endgame its stakes, because a colony that stops shipping cores is doing more than
  withholding revenue

**At least one export must be beautiful and grown rather than torn out of the ground.** Something the
planet's own life produces: renewable, tending it is a skill, the predecessors know how and the player
does not, and Earth's desperation for it does not require the world to be despoiled.

The full goods list, chain structure and depths are in the session, map and economy brief.

## 9. The predecessors, who replace the native layer

An earlier wave of settlers who came out under a worse charter and defaulted on it. They stayed because
going home meant the debt following them. They are the first ship's people and their descendants, the
ones who found Bloom and made the first cores. Going silent was their default: they stopped shipping
cores home and stopped answering.

- Human and immediately legible. No cultural translation required
- Here long enough to know how the ground behaves, and the player does not
- **The only place certain skills can be learned**, one person per settlement, ever
- They will not deal with the Company at all, which is why they trade only with the player, and in kind
- The relationship is competition and mutual need, never conquest

**Section 6 explains their competence.** They went furthest down the road of building only what they
can maintain, because they had no choice. Their skill is the consequence of their independence, which
is exactly the lesson the player is being taught.

Mechanically this is the native trade layer from the original, unchanged in shape: per-settlement
preferences, an anti-repetition rule, small transaction caps, no tax, rewards for routing and map
knowledge. Ethically it is a different object, because they are the player's predecessors rather than
the player's subjects.

## 10. The place is old

The world should feel lived in before anyone arrived, without that depth being carried by a people who
exist as a mechanic. Old workings, ruins, country that was clearly inhabited once. This gives the
predecessors something to have learned from, and the map a history the player can read.

The player also makes their own ruins, since exhausted gold diggings stay on the map.

## 12. Vocabulary direction

**Corrected 21 August 2026.** An earlier version of this section called for "period grammar" and a
Georgian register. That was wrong and it contradicted section 6. These are people from a civilisation
that crosses between worlds. They know what a transistor is. They would not talk like eighteenth
century colonists, and dressing them that way was costume rather than design.

**The register test: would a modern person, living in a place built of timber and iron, say this?**

An old word survives only where it is still the correct technical term for a thing these people
actually have. Attar is what a chemist calls a distilled floral oil. Cordage is what the rope trade
calls rope. Bastion, redoubt and palisade are what a military engineer calls those shapes. Those are
current in their fields and they stay.

Words that were fancy dress and have been removed: dray, factor, ropewalk, broadsheet, transportee,
bondsman, freeholder.

**What survives is the things, not the language.** Rope, sail, timber, iron, horses and gunpowder are
present because of section 6, not because of a century. An industrial base cannot be shipped, so a
colony builds what it can maintain, and what it can maintain is rope and sail and iron. That argument
is untouched by the correction.

Working vocabulary: the Company, the charter, the passage, the debt, the Landing. Grievance produced
in a meeting house and amplified by a press or a bulletin. Signatories, people of standing who put
their names to the cause.
The recall fleet. A consignment office that sells surplus without a ship. Haulers, outriders,
batteries. A standing ladder running **debtor, contracted, free, master**, which describes how much a
person still owes rather than a legal status borrowed from another century.

See the naming and vocabulary brief for the full list, and section 8.1 of the legal constraint for what
is prohibited.

## 14. If something here is wrong

Say so rather than working around it. Version 2 corrects one position taken in version 1: that the
Company simply did not sell machines. That was unenforceable across a year of travel and contradicted
by how technology actually diffuses. The replacement in section 6 is stronger because it turns the
same visual outcome into a repeated player decision rather than a restriction.

If a piece of this fiction is making a mechanic harder rather than more obvious, the fiction is the
thing that should give way.
